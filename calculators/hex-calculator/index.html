    Questions

    What is hexadecimal and why do engineers use it?

    Hexadecimal is base 16. It uses the digits 0 to 9 and the letters A to F, where A is 10 and F is 15. Every hex digit is exactly four binary bits (A = 1010, F = 1111), so a byte is always two hex digits. That makes hex the compact way to write register values, memory addresses, colours, MAC addresses and protocol bytes.

    How do you add hexadecimal numbers?

    Work from the right, one column at a time, as in decimal, but carry when a column reaches 16 instead of 10. A + F = 25 in decimal, which is 19 in hex: write 9 and carry 1. The calculator shows the carry row and each column, so 1A3 + 2F = 1D2 can be followed digit by digit.

    How does hexadecimal subtraction work, and what if the answer is negative?

    Subtract column by column from the right. When a column needs a bigger digit than it has, borrow 1 from the next column, which adds 16 to the current one. If B is larger than A, the calculator subtracts the smaller from the larger and puts a minus sign in front, so 2F − 1A3 = −174. That is sign-and-magnitude, not a two’s complement pattern.

    How are hex multiplication and division shown?

    Multiplication shows one partial product per digit of the multiplier, shifted left, then added. Division shows the long-division steps and reports both the quotient and the remainder, with the check Q × B + R = A. 64 ÷ 8 gives quotient C and remainder 4 (100 ÷ 8 = 12 remainder 4). Division truncates toward zero and the remainder takes the sign of the dividend.

    How large can the numbers be, and what is the difference between automatic width and a fixed width?

    Up to 1024 hex digits each, with up to 20 numbers for addition, subtraction and multiplication. The arithmetic is exact (arbitrary precision), so a long number never overflows or loses digits. Nothing is silently cut to 8, 16 or 32 bits. What is the difference between automatic width and a fixed width? Arithmetic mode never wraps: the result is as long as it needs to be. A fixed width models hardware. In Signed Hex and Bitwise you choose 8, 16, 32 or 64 bits, an operand that does not fit is refused, and two’s complement is applied only there. FF is 255 unsigned, but −1 in 8-bit two’s complement.

    How do I read a negative number in hexadecimal?

    Choose the width, then read the pattern as signed. If the top bit is 1 the value is negative: invert the bits, add 1, and read the result as the magnitude. 0xFF in 8 bits is −1, 0x80 is −128 and 0xFFFF in 16 bits is −1. The Signed Hex mode shows both readings, the minimum and maximum, and overflow and underflow warnings.

    Can I convert between hex, binary, decimal and octal?

    Yes. The converter shows all four at once, handles fractions (0.8 hex = 0.5 decimal) and negative numbers, and states when a fraction is cut because it does not end in the target base. If you type a number that could be in several bases, such as 11, it asks you to confirm the base instead of guessing.

    What do the bitwise operations do?

    AND keeps the bits set in both numbers, OR keeps bits set in either, XOR keeps bits that differ, and NOT flips every bit at the chosen width. Shifts move bits left or right; arithmetic right shift copies the sign bit, and rotates wrap the bits around. AA AND 0F = 0A is a mask that keeps the low nibble. AA OR 01 = AB sets bit 0.

    Which checksums are included?

    CRC-16/IBM (ARC), CRC-16/MODBUS, CRC-16/CCITT-FALSE and CRC-32, each with its polynomial, initial value, final XOR and reflection stated, plus a plain sum-8 and the two’s-complement sum (LRC) used by Intel HEX and Modbus ASCII. The sum is labelled as a sum: it is not a CRC. The standard check text 123456789 gives CBF43926 for CRC-32.

    Is anything sent to a server?

    No. The calculation runs in your browser, and the history list is stored only in your browser.
