What the discriminant tells you

For ax² + bx + c = 0 with a ≠ 0, the discriminant is Δ = b² − 4ac. It is the part under the root in the quadratic formula, x = (−b ± √Δ) / (2a), so its sign decides whether that root is real. Δ > 0 gives two distinct real roots, Δ = 0 one repeated real root (the parabola touches the x-axis at its vertex), and Δ < 0 two complex conjugate roots (the parabola does not meet the x-axis). The calculator does not stop at a negative Δ: it gives the complex pair.

When Δ is positive and the square of a rational number, the roots are rational and the quadratic factors over the rationals. When it is positive but not such a square, the roots are real and irrational.

Root analysis

Besides the type, the page works out what the coefficients say about the roots without solving for them one by one. The sum is −B/A and the product is C/A. The product decides the signs: a negative product means one root of each sign; a positive product means both roots share a sign, which is the sign of the sum. The sum of squares is (sum)² − 2(product). For real roots the distance between them is √Δ/|A|. For a complex pair the real part is −B/(2A), the imaginary part is ±√(−Δ)/(2|A|) and the modulus of each root is √(C/A).

Finding the coefficient for a type of root

A common question is: for which value of B or C does the equation have real roots, a repeated root or complex roots? Give two of A, B and C and the page solves Δ for the third and lists the range for each type, with exact boundaries. For x² + Bx + 9 = 0 the boundaries are B = ±6, where the roots coincide. For x² − 4x + C = 0 the boundary is C = 4.

Type of rootsx² + Bx + 9 = 0x² − 4x + C = 0
Two distinct real rootsB < −6 or B > 6C < 4
Two complex conjugate roots−6 < B < 6C > 4
One repeated real rootB = −6 or B = 6C = 4

When the unknown is A, the value A = 0 is left out of every range, because the equation is then linear and has no discriminant.

Exact discriminant, no tolerance

Every coefficient is held as an exact fraction of whole numbers, so Δ and its sign are exact. There is no tolerance below which a small Δ is called zero. A Δ of exactly zero is reported as a repeated root and any other Δ is not. This is the policy because a fixed tolerance such as 10⁻⁹ would misclassify equations whose coefficients are large or small. Entering 0.1, 0.2, 0.1 is exact: 0.1 is read as 1/10, not as a binary approximation, so Δ is exactly 0. When Δ is not zero but is less than a millionth of B², the page says the equation is close to a repeated root, because the type then depends on very small changes in the coefficients.

Questions

What does a discriminant of zero mean?

The two roots are equal, x = −B/(2A). The quadratic is a perfect square, A(x − r)², and the parabola touches the x-axis at its vertex.

Why are there two checks?

Each root is substituted into the equation in exact arithmetic and must give zero. Separately, (sum of roots)² − 4(product) must equal Δ/A², which ties the discriminant to the roots by a different route.

Can the discriminant be used for equations that are not in standard form?

Move everything to one side first so the equation reads ax² + bx + c = 0. For 2x² = 3x + 5 use A = 2, B = −3 and C = −5.

What happens when A is zero?

The equation is linear, so there is no discriminant. The page says so and gives the one root.

How do I use complex roots in a circuit calculation?

Mathematics writes the imaginary unit as i and electrical engineering writes it as j. A negative discriminant in a second-order circuit equation corresponds to an oscillating, under-damped response.
