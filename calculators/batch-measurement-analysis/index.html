The formulas

Error e = Reading − Reference

Mean signed error = Σ e ÷ n

Mean absolute error = Σ |e| ÷ n

Maximum absolute error = the largest |e|

Population standard deviation = √(Σ (Reading − mean)² ÷ n)

Sample standard deviation = √(Σ (Reading − mean)² ÷ (n − 1))

Root mean square error = √(Σ e² ÷ n)

The error follows the metrology convention of the International Vocabulary of Metrology: the measured value minus the reference value, so a reading above the reference is positive. Percentages divide by the size of the reference, 100 × value ÷ |reference|, exactly as in Modes 02 and 03. The standard deviation measures the spread of the readings about their own mean, so it does not depend on the reference at all.

Worked example: five readings against a reference of 230 V

StepWorking
Step 1Each error is the reading minus the reference 230 V: 229 − 230 = −1; 231 − 230 = +1; 230 − 230 = 0; 228 − 230 = −2; 232 − 230 = +2.
Step 2The sum of the readings is 1150 V, so the mean observed value is 1150 ÷ 5 = 230 V. The sum of the errors is 0 V, so the mean signed error is 0 ÷ 5 = 0 V: the readings are equal to the reference on average.
Step 3The sum of the absolute errors is 6 V, so the mean absolute error is 6 ÷ 5 = 1.2 V. The largest absolute error is 2 V at reading 4 (the first of 2 readings with that error).
Step 4The sum of the squared deviations of the readings from their mean is 10 V². The population standard deviation is √(10 ÷ 5) = ≈ 1.414214 V and the sample standard deviation is √(10 ÷ 4) = ≈ 1.581139 V.
Step 5Each percentage error is the error ÷ 230 × 100, for example (−1) ÷ 230 × 100 = ≈ −0.434783%. The mean signed deviation is 0% and the mean absolute percentage error is ≈ 0.521739%.
Step 6The entered tolerance ±5% of the reference gives the limits 218.5 V to 241.5 V (limits included). All 5 readings are within the entered tolerance.

Bias and spread are different things

The mean signed error tells you whether the readings sit above or below the reference on average. The standard deviation tells you how much they scatter. They answer different questions, and a batch can have one without the other.

The root mean square error combines the two: its square is the square of the bias plus the population variance. The page lists all three so that you can see where an error comes from.

Population or sample standard deviation

The population standard deviation divides by n and treats the readings as the complete set. The sample standard deviation divides by n − 1 and is the usual choice when the readings are a sample of repeated measurements. With one reading the sample standard deviation is not defined, and the page says so instead of showing a number. Square roots are rounded from an exact integer square root, so the last digit is correct. A square root that is a finite decimal, such as the 1 for readings 99, 101, 99 and 101 against 100, is shown exactly.

The tolerance is the one you enter

A tolerance is optional. If you enter one, every reading is checked against the limits Reference ± tolerance, with the limits included, and the page counts how many readings are inside, on a limit and outside, and lists the outside readings. The tolerance is whatever you type. This page does not know any standard, datasheet or procedure, so it never says that an instrument or a product is compliant, calibrated or approved. A pass means every reading is inside the tolerance you entered, nothing more. For a single reading with a lower and an upper tolerance of different sizes, use Mode 03.

Example: the seven readings of a power meter against 10 kW with an absolute tolerance of 0.3 kW give the limits 9.7 kW to 10.3 kW. 6 of 7 readings are within the entered tolerance; 1 is outside it.

The unit is a label. Nothing is converted, so enter the readings, the reference and an absolute tolerance in the same unit.

Negative and zero references

With a negative reference the percentages divide by its size. For −10 V a reading of −9 V is above the reference and has a positive error of +1 V, which is +10%. A reference of 0 is accepted here, unlike in Modes 01 and 02, because the errors, the means and the standard deviation are all still defined. The percentages are shown as not defined, and the chart plots the signed error in the unit instead of a percentage. A percentage tolerance needs a reference that is not 0, so use an absolute tolerance for a reading that should be zero, such as an offset.

Entering and importing readings

Type or paste the readings one per line, or separated by spaces, tabs or semicolons. A comma that ends a reading is accepted, so 229, 231, 230 works. A comma inside a number is refused, because 1,5 could be a decimal comma and 1,000 a thousand, and guessing would change your data. To import a file, choose a .csv or .txt file of up to 1 MB. If it has several columns, pick the column with the readings. The file is read in your browser and is not uploaded. Up to 1000 readings are analysed at once.

The deviation chart

The chart draws one bar for each reading: its signed percentage deviation, or its signed error in the unit when the reference is 0. The solid line is the reference, the dotted line is the mean signed error, and if you enter a tolerance two dashed lines mark its limits. A bar outside the tolerance is hatched and outlined, so it can be told apart without relying on colour. The table of individual readings holds the same numbers.

A standard deviation is not a measurement uncertainty

TermMeaningWhere it comes from
Measurement errorObserved value minus reference valueEach reading (Modes 01 and 02)
Mean signed error (bias)The average of the errors, keeping their signsThe batch of readings, on this page
Standard deviationThe spread of the readings about their own meanThe batch of readings, on this page
ToleranceThe permitted limits around a referenceA specification, datasheet, procedure or your own requirement. You enter it here (Mode 03)
Measurement uncertaintyThe dispersion of values that could reasonably be attributed to the measurandThe whole measurement process. Not calculated on this page
Accuracy, repeatability, conformityCloseness of agreement, spread of repeated readings, and a statement that a requirement is metDefined criteria and a defined method

The standard deviation of a few readings describes their scatter. It is not the uncertainty of the measurement, which also includes the reference, the instrument, the method and the conditions. A mean error of 2% does not mean that the instrument has 98% accuracy. Accuracy, repeatability and conformity need defined criteria that this page does not have. With few readings, the standard deviation is itself only a rough estimate.

Questions

What does a batch measurement analysis calculate?

For readings of one quantity against one reference it gives the average, minimum, maximum, mean signed error, mean absolute error, maximum absolute error, standard deviation, root mean square error and the percentage error of each reading. For the five readings 229, 231, 230, 228 and 232 V against 230 V the mean signed error is 0 V and the mean absolute error is 1.2 V.

What is the difference between mean signed error and mean absolute error?

The mean signed error keeps the sign, so errors above and below the reference cancel and it measures bias. The mean absolute error ignores the sign, so it measures the typical size of an error. In the example the first is 0 V and the second 1.2 V.

Should I use the population or the sample standard deviation?

Use the sample standard deviation, which divides by n − 1, when the readings are repeated measurements that stand for many possible ones. Use the population standard deviation, which divides by n, when the readings are the whole set. For the example they are ≈ 1.581139 V and ≈ 1.414214 V.

Does the standard deviation depend on the reference, and why is it shown with an approximate sign?

No. It is the spread of the readings about their own mean. Changing the reference changes the errors and the percentages but not the standard deviation. Why is the standard deviation shown with an approximate sign? Most standard deviations are square roots that never end. The page rounds the exact root half up to the decimal places you choose, six when automatic, and marks it as approximate. A root that ends, such as 1, is shown exactly.

Can I use a reference of 0?

Yes. The errors, means and standard deviation are still defined. The percentages are shown as not defined, and a tolerance has to be absolute.

How many readings can I enter?

Up to 1000. Enter them one per line or separated by spaces, tabs or semicolons, or import a .csv or .txt file.

How does the tolerance check work?

Enter a tolerance, as a percentage of the reference or an absolute value. Each reading is compared with the limits Reference ± tolerance, limits included. The page counts the readings inside and outside and lists the outside ones. With the seven power readings above, 6 of 7 readings are within the entered tolerance; 1 is outside it.

Does a pass mean the instrument is compliant or calibrated?

No. A pass means every reading is inside the tolerance you entered. The page does not read any standard or datasheet and does not apply measurement uncertainty.

Does the unit change the answer?

No. The unit is a label shown beside the values. The page does not convert between units, so enter everything in the same unit.

Is anything sent to a server?

No. Everything runs in your browser, and an imported file is read there. The history of your last ten analyses is kept only in this browser and can be cleared with one button.
