How a loan is amortized

An amortizing loan is repaid in equal instalments. Each month the interest is charged on the balance still owed, and whatever is left of the instalment reduces the balance. The regular monthly payment is EMI = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly rate (the annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100) and n is the number of monthly payments. Interest in month t is the opening balance × r, the principal part is the instalment minus that interest, and the closing balance is the opening balance minus the principal part. Working for a loan of $200,000 at 6 % for 15 years:

  1. Monthly rate r = 6 % ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.0050000000. Number of instalments n = 15 × 12 + 0 = 180.
  2. Regular instalment EMI = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n) = $200,000.00 × 0.0050000000 ÷ (1 − (1 + 0.0050000000)^−180) = $1,687.71 (rounded to the cent).
  3. Payment 1, due 1 Feb 2027: interest = opening balance × r = $200,000.00 × 0.0050000000 = $1,000.00; principal = instalment − interest = $1,687.71 − $1,000.00 = $687.71; closing balance = $200,000.00 − $687.71 = $199,312.29.
  4. Every later payment repeats this: interest is charged on the balance still owed, so the interest part falls and the principal part rises while the instalment stays $1,687.71.
  5. The final instalment (payment 180, 1 Jan 2042) is $1,688.73, adjusted so the balance ends at exactly zero. Total interest = total paid − principal = $303,788.82 − $200,000.00 = $103,788.82.

With no interest the instalment is simply the loan divided by the number of instalments. The final instalment is adjusted so the balance ends at exactly zero, which is why it can differ from the regular instalment by a few cents.

What extra payments do

An extra payment is credited to principal, so it removes the interest that balance would have earned for every remaining month. Because the instalment stays the same, the loan ends sooner.   3. Payment 1, due 15 Feb 2027: interest = opening balance × r = ₹50,00,000.00 × 0.0070833333 = ₹35,416.67; principal = instalment − interest = ₹43,391.16 − ₹35,416.67 = ₹7,974.49; closing balance = ₹50,00,000.00 − ₹7,974.49 − extra ₹5,000.00 = ₹49,87,025.51.
  4. Every later payment repeats this: interest is charged on the balance still owed, so the interest part falls and the principal part rises while the instalment stays ₹43,391.16.
  5. Extra principal (₹5,000.00 every month) is credited after that month’s regular instalment and reduces the balance at once. The instalment stays ₹43,391.16 and the loan ends sooner, after 187 payments instead of 240.
  6. The final instalment (payment 187, 15 Aug 2042) is ₹23,873.55, adjusted so the balance ends at exactly zero. Total interest = total paid − principal = ₹90,24,629.31 − ₹50,00,000.00 = ₹40,24,629.31.

Rules this calculator follows

  • Fixed rate, equal instalments. The rate does not change during the loan. A variable or floating rate needs a rate-reset schedule and is not part of this mode.
  • Interest on the opening balance. Each month’s interest is the balance at the start of the month × the annual rate ÷ 12, rounded half up to the cent. Day-count conventions such as actual/365 are not applied.
  • First payment one month after the start date, on the same day of the month, or on the last day when the month is shorter (a start on 31 January gives 28 February, 31 March, 30 April).
  • Extra payments reduce principal after the instalment of the same month. An extra payment larger than the balance is capped at the balance, and a one-time payment after the loan ends is ignored with a note.
  • No false precision. All money is held in whole cents, never in floating-point values, so totals add up exactly: principal repaid equals the loan amount, and total paid equals principal plus interest.
  • Checked every time. The result shows the checks it passed, including that the balance ends at zero and that the annual summary adds up to the monthly rows.
  • Not included: processing fees, insurance, taxes, prepayment penalties, moratorium or interest-only periods, and a lender’s own rounding or accrual rules. Compare with the lender’s schedule before relying on figures for a decision.

A loan with no interest

At 0 % the instalment is the loan divided by the number of payments: $120,000.00 over 10 years is $1,000.00 a month for 120 payments, with $0.00 of interest.

Questions

What is loan amortization?

Amortization is repaying a loan in regular instalments that cover the interest and reduce the balance, so the loan reaches zero on the last payment. The schedule shows, for every payment, how much is interest, how much is principal and what is still owed.

Why is most of the early payment interest?

Interest is charged on the balance still owed, which is highest at the start. As the balance falls, the interest part of the fixed instalment falls and the principal part rises. For $200,000 at 6 % over 15 years, the first payment is $1,000.00 interest and $687.71 principal.

How do extra payments save interest?

An extra payment reduces principal immediately, so that amount stops earning interest for the rest of the loan. The instalment stays the same, so the loan ends sooner. The calculator shows the interest saved and the time saved against the original schedule.

How is the monthly payment calculated?

EMI = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), with r the annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100 and n the number of months. At 0 % it is the loan divided by n. The result is rounded to the cent, and the final payment is adjusted to clear the balance.

Why might my lender’s schedule differ by a few cents or more?

Lenders can round differently, count days instead of months, add fees or insurance, change the rate during the loan or apply a different first-payment date. Use this schedule to understand the loan, and rely on the lender’s statement for exact figures.

When is the first payment due?

One month after the loan start date, on the same day of the month. If that month is shorter, the last day of the month is used.

What happens if my extra payment is more than the balance?

It is capped at the balance still owed, the loan ends that month, and the final instalment is reduced accordingly. A one-time payment set for a payment number after the loan has ended is ignored and the calculator says so.

Can I use it for a mortgage, car loan or personal loan?

Yes, for any fixed-rate loan repaid in equal monthly instalments. It does not include property tax, insurance, down payment or fees; those belong in a mortgage calculator. A later mode adds scenario comparison and export.

Is anything sent to a server?

The calculation runs in your browser. The history list is stored only in this browser and you can clear it. This page also loads Google Tag Manager; data collected by tags depends on its configuration.
