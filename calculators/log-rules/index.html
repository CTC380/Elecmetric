History

The logarithm rules

All the rules follow from one fact: log_b(x) = y means b^y = x. Adding exponents multiplies powers, so adding logarithms multiplies their arguments. Each rule needs every argument to be positive and every base to be positive and not 1.

Simplify and condense an expression

Enter a sum or difference of logarithms. A number in front of a logarithm moves inside as a power (power rule), a sum becomes a product (product rule), and a difference becomes a quotient (quotient rule). Terms whose bases are powers of one another, such as 2, 4 and 1/2, are first brought to one base by change of base, so log₂(3) + log₄(1/9) cancels to 0. Terms in unrelated bases, such as 2 and 3, are evaluated separately.

A result is called exact only when it is a rational number. The calculator decides this from the prime factors of the arguments and bases, never from the rounded decimal. When two or more groups with unrelated bases are irrational, the sum is shown as a decimal and the page says that exactness is not decided.

Expand a logarithm

Expanding is the reverse of condensing. The product rule splits log_b(xy), the quotient rule splits log_b(x/y), and the power rule brings an exponent down. The page shows the expansion as you typed the argument and then splits every factor into primes, so each term is as simple as possible.

There is no rule for the logarithm of a sum. When an argument contains a sum, such as 81/(2+7), the sum is worked out first and treated as one number.

Check a rule

Choose one of the nine rules, enter your own numbers, and the page calculates the left side and the right side separately, each in 90-digit arithmetic, and compares them. When both sides are rational the comparison is exact. When the values are irrational, as in log₃(5×7) = log₃(5) + log₃(7), the page says that they agree to better than 10^−70 and does not claim that the number is exact.

Use a minus sign or the − character. A comma is ignored, so 1,000 is read as 1000. Each term may hold one logarithm; there is no rule for a product of two logarithms. Full symbolic algebra, such as log(a*b) with letters, is not part of this mode.

Questions

Which expressions can it condense?

Sums and differences of numerical logarithms, each multiplied by a number, such as 2*log2(3) − log2(9) + log2(12). The arguments and bases can be whole numbers, decimals, fractions, powers, sums worked out first, or the constant e.

What if the bases are different?

If one base is a power of another (2, 4, 8, 1/2) the terms are converted to one base by change of base and combined. If the bases are unrelated (2 and 3) the groups are shown separately and added as decimals.

When is the answer exact?

When it is a rational number, such as log₂(8) + log₄(16) = 5 or log₂(3) + log₄(1/9) = 0. A sum such as log₂(3) + log₂(5) = log₂(15) is irrational, so only its decimal is given.

Why does it refuse log of a negative number?

A positive base raised to a real power is always positive, so a real logarithm exists only for a positive argument. The page gives the same messages as the Log Calculator: undefined for 0, no real result for a negative number, and an error for base 1.

Can I use letters like x and y?

Not in this mode. It works on numerical expressions so that every result can be calculated and checked. Symbolic expansion and simplification are planned as a later extension.

How is a result checked?

Each logarithm is recomputed as ln x ÷ ln b in 90-digit arithmetic, with every argument evaluated as one number, and compared with the condensed form. A double-precision calculation is a second cross-check, and exact results are confirmed by comparing prime exponents. The engine is tested against independent reference values from a computer algebra system.

Is anything sent to a server?

The calculation runs in your browser. The history list is stored only in this browser and you can clear it. This page also loads Google Tag Manager; data collected by tags depends on its configuration.
