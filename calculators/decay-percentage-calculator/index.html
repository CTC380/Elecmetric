    The formulas

    One decay law, Nₜ = N₀ × 2^(−t / T½), turned into percentages:

    To findFormulaValid when
    Half-lives elapsedn = t / T½t and T½ in the same unit; t ≥ 0, T½ > 0
    Fraction remainingf = 2^(−n) = (1/2)^nany n ≥ 0
    Remaining percentagef × 100%
    Fraction decayed1 − f = 1 − 2^(−n)
    Decayed percentage(1 − f) × 100% = 100% − remaining %
    Remaining quantityNₜ = N₀ × fN₀ > 0
    Decayed quantityN₀ − Nₜ = N₀ × (1 − f)N₀ > 0
    Time to fall to a percentage pt = T½ × log₂(100 / p)0 < p ≤ 100

    The percentage remaining depends only on how many half-lives have passed, not on the substance or the starting amount. That is why the initial quantity is optional.

    Rules and limits

    • The half-life must be greater than zero, and the elapsed time cannot be negative; zero is allowed and leaves 100%.
    • The initial quantity is optional; when entered it must be greater than zero.
    • The elapsed time and the half-life each have their own time unit, from nanoseconds to billions of years. A year is a Julian year of 365.25 days.
    • The quantity unit is a label only. Nothing is converted, and the percentages do not depend on it.
    • Results are double-precision. A remaining fraction below about 5 × 10⁻³²⁴ shows as 0% with a note that it is not exactly zero, and one below about 2.2 × 10⁻³⁰⁸ is flagged as carrying fewer digits.
    • Mathematical decay only: not a radiation-safety, dosimetry or exposure assessment.

    Questions

    How do I calculate the percentage remaining after a given time?

    Divide the elapsed time by the half-life to get the number of half-lives n, then the fraction left is 2^(−n). For a 10-year half-life and 20 years that is n = 2 and 25% left.

    Do I have to enter an initial quantity, and why is a zero initial quantity refused?

    No. The percentages do not depend on it. Leave it empty to see percentages only, or enter it to also get the remaining and decayed quantity in whatever unit you label it with. Why is a zero initial quantity refused? A percentage of nothing is undefined, and the remaining quantity would be 0 whatever the time. Leave the field empty if you only want the percentages.

    What if the elapsed time is not a whole number of half-lives, and can the units differ?

    The same formula works. After 0.3 half-lives (3 years of a 10-year half-life) 81.2252% is left. Do not interpolate between the halving steps; the decay is smooth, not stepped. Can the elapsed time and the half-life be in different units? Yes. Pick a unit for each. The elapsed time is converted to the unit of the half-life first, using exact unit ratios (a year is 365.25 days). Three days with a 12 hour half-life is 6 half-lives, and 1.5625% is left.

    Why is 100 g after three half-lives exactly 12.5 g?

    Because three halvings give 100 × (1/2)³ = 12.5, and the calculator computes powers of two directly rather than through a logarithm, so whole numbers of half-lives are exact: 12.5 g, 12.5%.

    What is the decayed percentage, and why does it never reach 100%?

    The part of the original that has gone: 100% minus the remaining percentage. After one half-life it is 50%, after two 75%, after three 87.5%. Why does the decayed percentage never reach 100%? It approaches 100% but never reaches it in finite time. Each half-life removes half of what is left, so some always remains. After 100 half-lives the fraction left is 7.88861 × 10⁻²⁹%, tiny but not zero. For a count of whole atoms the last one decays at a random moment, which is a statistical statement the model does not make.

    What happens at an elapsed time of zero?

    Nothing has decayed: n = 0, 100% remains and 0% has decayed. The calculator accepts zero for the elapsed time and refuses a negative one.

    How accurate are very small decayed percentages?

    For a short time next to the half-life the decayed fraction is computed as −expm1(−n ln 2) rather than 1 − 2^(−n), which would round to zero. A time of one billionth of a half-life gives a decay of 6.93147 × 10⁻¹⁰, not 0.

    Does this give the time needed to reach a given percentage?

    The table under the results shows how long it takes to fall to 90%, 75%, 50%, 25%, 10%, 5%, 1% and 0.1% for the half-life you entered. For any other target use the Half-Life Calculator (Mode 01), which solves for the elapsed time.

    Is this a radiation safety or dose calculator?

    No. It evaluates one exponential law. It does not give a dose, an exposure or a safety assessment, and it ignores any growth from a parent nuclide.

    Is anything sent to a server?

    No. The calculation runs in your browser, and the history list is stored only in your browser.
