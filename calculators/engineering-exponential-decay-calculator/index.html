    The formulas

    The same decay law as the other modes, written with the time constant τ:

    To findFormulaNotes
    RC time constantτ = R CR in ohm, C in farad, τ in seconds
    RC voltageV(t) = V₀ e^(−t / RC)discharge to zero through R
    RC half-value timet½ = R C ln 2about 0.693 τ
    RL time constantτ = L / RL in henry, R in ohm
    RL currentI(t) = I₀ e^(−t R / L)source removed, loop closed through R
    RL half-value timet½ = (L / R) ln 2about 0.693 τ
    Time to a targett = τ ln(x₀ / x(t))0 < x(t) ≤ x₀
    Decay rateλ = 1 / τ = ln 2 / t½the same λ as in Mode 02
    Energy in C or L½ C V² or ½ L I²falls as e^(−2t / τ), twice as fast as V or I

    Radioactive half-life and RC / RL half-value time are not the same process

    They share one equation and the relations λ = 1 / τ and t½ = τ ln 2. They are different physics, and they should not be mixed up.

    ProcessLawHalf-life or half-value timeWho sets it
    Radioactive decayNₜ = N₀ 2^(−t / T½)T½ is a property of the nuclideCannot be changed by the user
    RC capacitor dischargeV(t) = V₀ e^(−t / RC)t½ = R C ln 2Set by the resistor and capacitor you choose
    RL current decayI(t) = I₀ e^(−t R / L)t½ = (L / R) ln 2Set by the coil and the loop resistance
    Signal or degradationx(t) = x₀ e^(−t / τ)τ measured or assumedA model of your system, not a law of nature

    Rules and limits

    • Initial voltage, current, value, resistance, capacitance, inductance, time constant and half-life must be greater than zero. Time may be zero but not negative.
    • A target must be greater than zero and not more than the initial value (or 100%). A target of exactly zero is refused: it has no finite time.
    • The model is a first-order circuit discharging to zero: no leakage, ESR, dielectric absorption, coil capacitance, saturation or switching arc. For a source-driven transient or a series RLC circuit use the RC and RL Transient and RLC Transient pages.
    • Units are converted to SI exactly (prefixes by powers of ten). The voltage, current or value is shown in the unit you entered.
    • Results are double-precision. A remaining fraction below about 5 × 10⁻³²⁴ shows as 0% with a note that it is not exactly zero.
    • Mathematical results only: not a safety assessment, and not a radiation-safety, dosimetry or exposure assessment.

    Questions

    What does this calculator do?

    It evaluates one exponential law for three cases. For an RC circuit it gives V(t) = V₀ e^(−t/RC); for an RL circuit I(t) = I₀ e^(−tR/L); for any other process you give τ or a half-life. You can find the value at a time, or the time to reach a target.

    How do I discharge-time a capacitor, for example 100 V down to 50 V?

    Choose RC, enter 100 V, 10 kΩ and 100 µF, choose “The time to reach a target” and enter 50 V. The time constant is 1 s and the time is τ ln 2 = 693.147 ms.

    What is the value after one time constant, and how does the time constant differ from the half-value time?

    About 36.8% of the initial value is left: 100 V falls to 36.7879 V after one τ. After five time constants 0.673795% is still left, so a circuit is never exactly discharged in finite time. What is the difference between the time constant and the half-value time? The time constant τ is the time to fall to 1/e (36.8%). The half-value time t½ = τ ln 2 ≈ 0.693 τ is the time to fall to 50%. They describe the same curve. In the 10 kΩ, 100 µF circuit τ is 1 s and t½ is 693.147 ms.

    Is this the same as radioactive half-life?

    The equation is the same, with λ = 1 / τ, but the process is different. A nuclide’s half-life is a fixed property of the nuclide. The half-value time of a circuit depends on the components you chose, and changes when you change R, C or L.

    Which resistance do I enter for an RL circuit, and what about opening a switch in series with an inductor?

    The total resistance of the loop the current flows round while it decays: the discharge resistor plus the coil’s own winding resistance plus any diode or switch drop you want to include as a resistance. For a coil of 2 H in a 22 Ω loop the time constant is 90.9091 ms. What about opening a switch in series with an inductor? That is not covered. If there is no path for the current, the voltage rises until something breaks down (an arc, a diode, a transient suppressor). This calculator assumes the current decays through a resistive path, such as a freewheel diode and resistor or a discharge resistor.

    Does it include leakage, ESR or dielectric absorption, and can I use it to decide a capacitor is safe to touch?

    No. It is the ideal first-order model: a capacitor with no leakage or series resistance beyond the R you enter, and an ideal inductor. A real capacitor can recover some voltage after being shorted (dielectric absorption), and an electrolytic capacitor has leakage that shortens the decay. Can I use it to decide a capacitor is safe to touch? No. It is a calculation, not a safety assessment. Use the calculated time as a planning figure only, then measure the voltage with a meter rated for it and discharge with a proper tool. Stored energy in a high-voltage capacitor can be dangerous at voltages that the formula says are “low”.

    What is the generic mode for?

    For a signal envelope, an exponential attenuation model or the degradation of any quantity. Enter the initial value, and either a time constant or a half-life. Only the numbers are used: the unit is a label.

    How is the time to a target calculated?

    t = τ ln(x₀ / x). For a target of 10% that is τ ln 10 = 2.3026 τ. A target of exactly zero is refused, because it is only reached at infinite time; use a small positive target such as 1% instead.

    What can I download?

    The time-constant ladder and the fall-to-a-level table as CSV, TXT or JSON, and the graph as SVG or PNG. The saved graph has a white background and fixed colours so it can go into a report or a slide.

    Is anything sent to a server?

    No. The calculation and the graph are made in your browser, and the history list is stored only in your browser.
