Why a quadratic describes an RLC circuit

Write Kirchhoff’s laws for a circuit with one inductor and one capacitor and you get a second-order differential equation. Trying a solution of the form est turns it into an ordinary quadratic in s, the characteristic equation. Its two roots are the poles of the system, and they decide everything about the natural response: whether it dies away, whether it rings and how fast. This page solves that quadratic with the same exact arithmetic as Modes 01 to 04 and then turns the roots into the numbers an engineer wants.

The unknown x(t) in the response stands for any quantity that obeys the same equation: the inductor current or the capacitor voltage of a series circuit, the node voltage of a parallel circuit, the displacement of the mass. You choose its starting value and slope.

Overdamped, critically damped and underdamped

Write the equation as s² + 2αs + ω₀² = 0. The neper frequency α = b/(2a) measures the loss, and ω₀ = √(c/a) is the undamped natural frequency. The damping ratio is ζ = α/ω₀. The sign of the discriminant, b² − 4ac, which is 4(α² − ω₀²) a, sorts the response: positive gives two real roots and a response that creeps back without oscillating (ζ > 1, overdamped); zero gives one repeated root and the fastest return without overshoot (ζ = 1, critically damped); negative gives a complex pair and a ringing response (ζ < 1, underdamped). With no loss at all, ζ = 0 and the ringing never fades.

Q, bandwidth and resonance

The damping ratio and the quality factor are two views of the same thing: Q = 1/(2ζ) = ω₀/(2α), and the −3 dB bandwidth is ω₀/Q = 2α. For a series circuit Q = (1/R)√(L/C) and the bandwidth is R/L. For a parallel circuit Q = R√(C/L) and the bandwidth is 1/(RC). The parallel circuit of 1 kΩ, 10 mH and 100 nF has Q = 3.1623 and resonates at 5.03292 kHz. The page also gives the half-power frequencies, f₁ and f₂, whose geometric mean is the resonant frequency.

Control systems and the step overshoot

A second-order control loop in the standard form has the same characteristic equation with 2ζωₙ for b. When the system is stable and underdamped, the unit-step response overshoots its final value by 100·e−πζ/√(1−ζ²) percent, at a time π/ωd. For ζ = 0.5 that is 16.3 %, at 36.276 ms when ωₙ = 100 rad/s. A negative coefficient, or a coefficient that is zero, changes the stability: the page reads the signs exactly and says whether the roots lie in the left half-plane, on the imaginary axis or in the right half-plane.

Mass-spring-damper

A mass m on a spring k with a viscous damper c obeys m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = 0, so the same quadratic applies. The critical damping is c = 2√(km); for m = 1, k = 4 that is 4 N·s/m, and the page confirms that c = 4 is then exactly critical.

Exact, not rounded

Every value is read as an exact fraction, including SI prefixes: 4.7k, 100 nF and 2.2u are 4700, 1/10 000 000 and 11/5 000 000 exactly. The class is then decided from the exact discriminant, so a circuit sitting on the boundary is reported as critically damped, and one a single part in 10¹⁶ either side is not. A floating-point calculation cannot make that distinction. The frequencies, times and the response are then evaluated in double precision from the exact discriminant, and each is checked against the differential equation itself.

Questions

What is the characteristic equation of a series RLC circuit?

L·s² + R·s + 1/C = 0, or s² + (R/L)·s + 1/(LC) = 0 after dividing by L. Its roots are the poles of the circuit. For the example of 10 Ω, 1 mH and 100 µF they are −5000 − 1000√15 and −5000 + 1000√15 per second.

How do I know if the circuit is overdamped, critically damped or underdamped?

Look at the discriminant of the characteristic equation. For a series circuit it is R² − 4L/C (after scaling): positive means overdamped, zero means critically damped, negative means underdamped. This page decides it exactly.

What resistance gives critical damping?

For a series circuit R = 2√(L/C). For a parallel circuit R = ½√(L/C). For a mass-spring-damper c = 2√(km). With L = 1 mH and C = 10 µF the series value is 20 Ω.

Can I type values with units?

Yes. Prefixes p, n, µ (or u), m, k, M and G are understood, and the unit symbol may follow: 4.7kΩ, 100 nF, 10 mH. Fractions and scientific notation also work.

What if a coefficient is negative?

Negative physical values are refused for R, L, C, mass and stiffness. In the general control form, any sign is allowed, and the page tells you whether the system is stable, marginally stable or unstable.
