Limits of this calculator

Use this to see how many counts a sensor really gets, whether the converter is enough, and whether the sampling rate is safe. Fewer counts than you expected usually means the span uses only part of the range: scale the signal to fill it. The ENOB, not the bit count, tells what the converter can do, so check the SINAD at your signal frequency on the data sheet. Sampling just above twice the highest frequency leaves nothing for filter roll-off, so practical systems sample at 5 to 10 times the highest frequency, or filter before the converter.

What this calculator checks

  • The step size FSR / 2^N, the number of levels, the ideal count for an input voltage (unipolar and bipolar ranges), the maximum quantisation error and the ideal full-scale SNR, against independent vectors written from the transition levels of the converter and the signal and noise powers.
  • The effective number of bits from a SINAD, the counts, percentage of range, resolution in bits and units per count for a sensor span inside the input range, and the largest gain that keeps the span in range, including the 3.3 V and 4 to 20 mA worked examples of the ADC guide.
  • The Nyquist frequency, oversampling ratio, the extra resolution from oversampling, and the alias frequency of a tone above half the sampling rate found by folding it about fs / 2.
  • The refusal of a fractional bit count, a sensor span that is not inside the input range or has its high value at or below its low value, and a SINAD of 1.76 dB or less.

What remains unverified

  • Offset, gain, integral and differential non-linearity, missing codes, reference and input-buffer errors, and the sampling-switch and aperture effects of a real converter; only the SINAD you enter brings noise and distortion in, as the ENOB.
  • The behaviour of the anti-alias filter (its order, corner and the attenuation it gives at the alias), and of sigma-delta, pipelined or successive-approximation architectures: the converter is taken as an ideal quantiser.
  • The oversampling gain assumes white, quantisation-limited noise and is not valid when converter noise, jitter or the reference dominates; noise shaping is not modelled.
  • Sample-and-hold settling, multiplexer sharing of the sampling rate between channels, clock jitter and the data sheet test conditions behind the SINAD entered.

ADC Resolution and Sampling Formula

LSB = FSR / 2^N counts = floor((V − V0) / LSB) quantisation error ≤ ½ LSB\nIdeal SNR = 6.02 × N + 1.76 dB (full-scale sine) ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76) / 6.02\nNyquist: fs > 2 × fmax oversampling ratio = fs / (2 × fmax), extra bits ≈ ½ × log2(OSR)\nAlias of a tone f above fs / 2: |f − k × fs| with k the whole number nearest f / fs

Assumptions and Limitations

  • An ideal converter with a straight transfer line from code 0 to code 2^N − 1 over the full-scale range; no offset, gain, linearity or noise error except where SINAD is entered
  • The ideal SNR uses a full-scale sine wave and quantisation noise of LSB² / 12; the constants are 6.0206 and 1.7609, shown as 6.02 and 1.76
  • The oversampling gain assumes the noise is white and the converter noise is quantisation-limited; it is not available if the converter noise or a reference is the limit
  • The alias is for a single tone with no anti-alias filter; a real filter attenuates it but does not remove it

How to Calculate ADC Resolution and Sampling

Each input is converted to the base unit of its quantity, the formula above is applied in the order shown in the worked example, and each result is shown in the unit you choose. Results are held at full precision and rounded only for display, except where a step selects a standard size, as the worked example shows. A value outside its allowed range is refused with a message instead of giving a result.
