Limits of this calculator

Use this to see what an unbalanced or unequal load does: the currents in each line, how much current the neutral carries, how far the star point moves when there is no neutral, and what the line impedance costs in voltage and loss. For a balanced load all three line currents are equal, the neutral current is zero and the power is √3 × VL × IL × pf, which the Three-Phase Power calculator gives directly. For an unbalanced star load with no neutral wire, one phase can end up with a voltage well above the phase voltage and another well below it, so a lightly loaded phase on a three-wire star can be overvoltaged, which is why a neutral is carried to an unbalanced star load. With the neutral connected, the neutral carries the phasor sum of the three line currents, which can be as large as the largest line current. The calculation is an exact phasor solution of the circuit you describe. For the effect of harmonics on a neutral, a real network with sources and cables of different impedance, or a fault, use a load flow or a short-circuit study. For the line and phase quantities of a balanced load see the Line and Phase Quantities calculator, for the neutral current from three measured currents the Neutral Current from Unbalanced Load calculator, and for the sequence components of a measured set the Symmetrical Components calculator.

What this calculator checks

  • A balanced three-phase supply (line-to-line voltage entered, phase order a-b-c, phase a as the angle reference) feeding a load of three independent impedances, each a resistance and a reactance, connected in star with the neutral wire, in star with no neutral wire, or in delta, with an optional equal impedance in each line and an optional neutral-wire impedance (0 for a solid neutral).
  • An exact steady-state phasor solution: the line currents with their angles, the neutral current, the shift of the load star point (the neutral-point voltage of a star load), the delta branch currents and voltages, the voltage and current in each load impedance, the line voltages at the load, the real, reactive and apparent power of the load and of the supply, the loss in the line and neutral wires (with a power-balance check), the supply and load power factor, the current unbalance, the negative-sequence share of the current and the voltage unbalance and drop at the load.
  • Warnings when the star point of a three-wire star moves more than 10% of the phase voltage, when the neutral current is larger than the largest line current, when the current unbalance is above 10%, when the line voltage unbalance at the load is above 2%, when the average line voltage at the load is more than 5% below the supply or more than 2% above it, and a rejection of a zero impedance, of a delta whose impedances add to zero, and of a phase that resonates with the line impedance.

What remains unverified

  • The supply impedance, an unbalanced or distorted supply voltage, a different impedance in each line, mutual coupling between the lines, a delta supply with a grounded corner and the earth return of a fault: the source is a perfect balanced set and the network is the single load and line impedance entered.
  • Harmonics and non-linear loads (the neutral current of triplen harmonics is not modelled), the change of a real load with voltage (motors and constant-power loads are not constant impedances), and the frequency dependence of the impedances: every impedance is taken as the constant resistance and reactance entered at one frequency.
  • Equipment ratings, the cable size and its temperature, the protective device, the standards limits on neutral size, voltage drop and voltage unbalance, and a fault or a switching condition: the 10%, 2% and 5% warning levels are common screening values, not limits of any one standard, and the result is not a network study.

Three-Phase Circuit Formula

Star: Ik = (Ek − Vn) / (Zk + Zl) Vn = (Σ Ek / (Zk + Zl)) / (Σ 1 / (Zk + Zl) + 1 / Zn) delta to star: Za = Zab Zca / ΣZ, Zb = Zab Zbc / ΣZ, Zc = Zbc Zca / ΣZ delta branch: Jab = Vab / Zab In = Ia + Ib + Ic S = Σ |J|² Z I1, I2 = (Ia + a Ib + a² Ic) / 3, (Ia + a² Ib + a Ic) / 3

Assumptions and Limitations

  • A balanced three-phase supply of the line-to-line voltage entered, phase order a-b-c, with phase a as the angle reference: Van = VL / √3 ∠0°, Vbn = ∠−120°, Vcn = ∠+120°. The supply impedance is not modelled; enter it as part of the line impedance if it matters
  • The load is three linear impedances of constant resistance and reactance at one frequency (sinusoidal, steady state). A constant-power load such as a motor at changing voltage is not a constant impedance, so use the result as the answer for the impedances entered
  • Star: the impedances run from each line to the load star point. With the neutral wire, the star point is joined to the supply neutral through the neutral impedance, and 0 + j0 is an ideal solid neutral. Without it the star point floats and moves to a voltage set by the unbalance. Delta: the impedances sit between lines a-b, b-c and c-a
  • The line impedance is the same in all three lines and is in series between the supply and the load. For delta the load is first turned into its equivalent star so the line impedance can be added, and the delta branch voltages and currents are then found from the line voltages at the load terminals
  • Power is the real and reactive power in the load impedances (|J|² R and |J|² X, reactive positive for an inductive load). Supply power is the power leaving the source, so supply real power equals the load real power plus the loss in the line and neutral wires, which is shown as a check in the working
  • Current unbalance is the largest deviation of a line current from the average line current as a percentage of that average (the NEMA-style definition used for voltage); the negative-sequence share |I2| / |I1| uses the symmetrical-component transformation. Both are screening figures. Voltage unbalance is worked out the same way on the three line voltages at the load
  • The warning levels (10% current unbalance, 2% voltage unbalance, 5% voltage drop, 10% movement of the load star point) are common screening values, not limits of one standard

What does this calculator assume?

A constant-power load such as a motor at changing voltage is not a constant impedance, so use the result as the answer for the impedances entered. Star: the impedances run from each line to the load star point. Without it the star point floats and moves to a voltage set by the unbalance. Delta: the impedances sit between lines a-b, b-c and c-a.
