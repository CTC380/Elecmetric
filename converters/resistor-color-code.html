R = (12 V - 2 V) / 20 mA = 500 Ω, which is not a standard E24 value. The next value above is 510 Ω. With 510 Ω the current is 19.61 mA, 1.96% below the target.

Ohm’s Law check: the resistor drops 10 V (the supply less the LED voltage). I = V / R = 10 V / 510 Ω = 19.61 mA. P = V × I = 10 V × 19.61 mA = 196.1 mW.

This is the resistor only. The forward voltage changes with current, temperature and the individual LED, the supply may not be exactly the stated voltage, and the LED’s own maximum current and the resistor’s datasheet rating are the limits. Check the result against both datasheets.

Ohm’s Law check: I = Vin / (R1 + R2) = 12 V / (5.6 kΩ + 3.9 kΩ) = 1.263 mA. Across R1: V = I × R = 1.263 mA × 5.6 kΩ = 7.074 V. Across R2: 1.263 mA × 3.9 kΩ = 4.926 V. Together 12 V, the input voltage.

Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2); with a load, R2 is taken in parallel with it. Lower resistances hold the output steadier against a load but draw more current from the supply. The tolerance range uses the series tolerance on both resistors at once, in opposite directions, so it is the worst case for the nominal values; a divider also changes with temperature and with any current the load draws that you have not entered. Check the resistor datasheets.

Tolerance impact: LED current

A resistor of a stated tolerance does not fix the LED current: the supply and the LED voltage move it too. Enter the tolerances to see the lowest and highest current, which source matters most, and the resistor power in the worst case. Blank tolerances count as 0.

Nominal current = (12 V − 2 V) / 510 Ω = 19.61 mA. Worst case: the supply at −5% with the LED voltage up by 0.2 V and the resistor at +5% gives the lowest current; the supply at +5% with the LED voltage down by 0.2 V and the resistor at −5% gives the highest. The largest single source is: supply tolerance. In the worst case the LED gets 22.29 mA, above its 20 mA limit: use a larger resistor, a tighter supply or check the LED’s rating.

The worst case puts every quantity at its limit at once, so it is a bound, not an expectation. The typical spread is the root-sum-square of the three single-source deviations, a common estimate when the errors are independent. The LED voltage also changes with current and temperature, and the resistor with temperature and age: check the datasheets, and measure the finished circuit where it matters.

Frequently Asked Questions
How do I read a 4 band resistor?
The first two bands are digits, the third is the multiplier and the fourth is the tolerance. Yellow, violet, red, gold is 4, 7, ×100 and ±5%, which is 4,700 Ω (4.7 kΩ) ±5%.
How do I know which end to start from?
Start from the end that the first band is nearest to: IEC 60062 says that, wherever possible, the first band is placed nearer its end of the resistor than the last band is to the other end. The tolerance band is read last; it is made at least 1.5 times as wide as the other bands and is often gold or silver, so it is set slightly apart. If you cannot tell, measure the resistor with a meter.
What is the difference between 4, 5 and 6 band resistors?
A 5 band resistor has a third digit, so it can show more exact values (precision resistors). A 6 band resistor adds a temperature coefficient band, in ppm per kelvin (IEC 60062:2016: black 250, brown 100, red 50, orange 15, yellow 25, green 20, blue 10, violet 5, grey 1). The 2016 edition no longer makes the sixth band wider, so the tolerance band stays the single wider band. A 3 band resistor has no tolerance band and is ±20%.
Which colour means what?
Black 0, brown 1, red 2, orange 3, yellow 4, green 5, blue 6, violet 7, grey 8, white 9. As a multiplier gold is ×0.1, silver is ×0.01 and pink is ×0.001 (pink was added in IEC 60062:2016 with Amendment 1:2019, so older charts do not show it). As a tolerance gold is ±5% and silver is ±10%; the precision colours are brown ±1%, red ±2%, green ±0.5%, blue ±0.25%, violet ±0.1%, and, in the 2016 edition, orange ±0.05%, yellow ±0.02% and grey ±0.01% (older charts give grey as ±0.05%, so check the datasheet for an older part).
How do I read an SMD resistor code such as 472 or 1002?
On a 3-digit code the first two digits are the value and the third is the power of ten, so 472 is 47 × 10² = 4,700 Ω (4.7 kΩ) and 103 is 10 kΩ. A 4-digit code uses three digits and a power of ten, so 1002 is 100 × 10² = 10 kΩ.
What does 4R7 mean on a resistor?
The R is the decimal point, so 4R7 is 4.7 Ω and R100 is 0.1 Ω. K and M work the same way for kilohms and megohms: 4K7 is 4.7 kΩ and 1M2 is 1.2 MΩ. L is the milliohm and G the gigaohm (IEC 60062:2016 Table 2): 4L7 is 4.7 mΩ, L100 is 0.1 mΩ and 1G0 is 1 GΩ. Values with four significant digits use five characters: 59R04 is 59.04 Ω and 5K904 is 5.904 kΩ.
How do I read an EIA-96 code such as 10B?
Two digits from 01 to 96 pick one of the 96 values of the E96 series (code 10 is 124) and the letter is the multiplier (B is ×10), so 10B is 1.24 kΩ. The code table on this page lists all 96 values and the letters.
What is the nearest standard resistor value?
Resistors are made in preferred values (IEC 60063 E series), not every number. E12 has 12 values per decade, E24 has 24, E96 has 96. For example 500 Ω is not a standard value: the nearest E24 value is 510 Ω (+2%), and in E12 it is 470 Ω (−6%). The finder on this page shows the nearest value and the next value on each side, so you can round up or down on purpose.
How do I find the total of resistors in series or parallel?
In series the values add: 330 Ω + 180 Ω = 510 Ω. In parallel the total is 1 / (1/R1 + 1/R2 + …): two 1 kΩ resistors give 500 Ω, and the total is always below the smallest one. Enter the values in the calculator on this page and it also shows the nearest standard value of the series you choose.
What power rating does a resistor need?
Work out the power it dissipates, P = V² / R or I² × R, then choose a rating well above it. With 10 V across 510 Ω the power is about 196 mW, and keeping to half the rating needs at least 392 mW, so a 1/2 W resistor is the next common size. The colour code does not show the power rating; it comes from the size of the part and the datasheet, and it falls at higher ambient temperatures.
How do I choose a resistor for an LED?
Subtract the LED forward voltage from the supply and divide by the current you want: (12 V − 2 V) / 20 mA = 500 Ω. That is not a standard value, so take the next standard value above (510 Ω in E24), which gives about 19.6 mA and never more than you asked for, then read its colour bands (green, brown, brown, gold) and check the power: 10 V × 19.6 mA is about 196 mW, so a 1/2 W resistor with margin. The forward voltage changes with current, temperature and LED colour, so take it from the datasheet.
How much can tolerance change an LED current or a divider output?
More than the resistor tolerance alone suggests. With 12 V ±5%, a 2 V LED that can shift by 0.2 V and a 510 Ω ±5% resistor, the current is 19.61 mA nominal but can range from 17.18 mA to 22.29 mA in the worst case, and the supply is the largest single source. The tolerance impact cards on this page show the lowest and highest result, each source on its own, and a root-sum-square estimate of the typical spread.
Does the SMD code show the tolerance or the power rating?
No. The marking gives the resistance only. The tolerance and the power rating come from the datasheet or the size and series of the part.
