    The formulas

    One decay law, Nₜ = N₀ × 2^(−t / T½) = N₀ × e^(−λt), solved four ways:

    To findFormulaValid when
    Remaining quantity NₜNₜ = N₀ × 2^(−t / T½)N₀ > 0, T½ > 0, t ≥ 0
    Initial quantity N₀N₀ = Nₜ × 2^(t / T½)Nₜ > 0, T½ > 0, t ≥ 0
    Elapsed time tt = T½ × log₂(N₀ / Nₜ) = T½ × ln(N₀ / Nₜ) / ln 20 < Nₜ ≤ N₀, T½ > 0
    Half-life T½T½ = t × ln 2 / ln(N₀ / Nₜ) = t / log₂(N₀ / Nₜ)0 < Nₜ < N₀, t > 0
    Decay constant λλ = ln 2 / T½T½ > 0
    Mean lifetime ττ = 1 / λ = T½ / ln 2T½ > 0

    Elapsed time and half-life use logarithms of the ratio N₀ / Nₜ, computed so that very large and very small amounts do not overflow or lose precision.

    One law, several different processes

    The mathematics is shared, but the physical meaning is not. A radioactive half-life, a biological half-life and the half-value time of an RC or RL circuit describe different processes and must not be mixed.

    Rules and limits

    • The initial quantity and the half-life must be greater than zero, and the elapsed time cannot be negative.
    • To find elapsed time or half-life, the remaining quantity must be greater than zero and no greater than the initial quantity.
    • A remaining quantity of exactly zero is not treated as a finite-time answer.
    • Elapsed time and half-life share one time unit; the quantity unit is a label only.
    • Results are double-precision. A remaining amount below about 5 × 10⁻³²⁴ shows as 0 with a note, and an initial amount above about 1.8 × 10³⁰⁸ shows as infinite with a note.
    • Mathematical decay only: not a radiation-safety, dosimetry or exposure assessment.

    Questions

    How do I calculate how much is left after a given time?

    Divide the elapsed time by the half-life to get the number of half-lives n, then halve the starting amount n times: Nₜ = N₀ × 2^(−n). For 100 g with a half-life of 5,730 years after 11,460 years, n = 2 and 25 g remains.

    Can n be a number that is not whole?

    Yes. After 1.5 half-lives, 35.3553 g of 100 g remains. The halving ladder is only the whole-number cases of the same law, and the calculator accepts any elapsed time.

    How do I find the half-life from two measurements?

    Use T½ = t × ln 2 / ln(N₀ / Nₜ). A quantity that fell from 100 to 25 in 11,460 years has N₀ / Nₜ = 4, which is two half-lives, so T½ = 5,730 years.

    Why does the calculator refuse a remaining quantity of zero or larger than the initial quantity, and what if it equals the initial quantity?

    Exponential decay never reaches exactly zero in finite time, so there is no elapsed time or half-life to report. Enter a small positive amount, for example one atom or the detection limit, to get a finite answer. Why is a remaining quantity larger than the initial quantity refused? This is ordinary decay, in which the amount can only fall. A larger remaining amount would mean growth or an added source, which this model does not describe. What if the remaining quantity equals the initial quantity? With a non-zero elapsed time no decay was observed, so the half-life is not finite and the page says so. With the half-life known, the elapsed time is 0.

    What is the decay constant, and how is it related to the half-life?

    λ = ln 2 / T½ is the fraction removed per unit time in the continuous form Nₜ = N₀ e^(−λt). For a 10-year half-life λ = 0.0693147 per year, and the mean lifetime τ = T½ / ln 2 = 14.427 years.

    Which time and quantity units can I use?

    Nanoseconds to billions of years. Elapsed time and half-life share one unit, chosen once, so they cannot be mixed by mistake. Years are Julian years of 365.25 days. Convert the numbers yourself if your half-life is in a different unit from your elapsed time. What quantity units can I use? Any label: grams, becquerels, atoms, percent or your own. The label is shown with the results and nothing is converted. N₀ and Nₜ must be in the same unit, because only their ratio matters.

    Does the uncertainty of a measured half-life carry through?

    No. The calculator treats every number as exact. A half-life measured as 5,730 ± 40 years gives a range of results, not one, and uncertainty in the measured amounts is a separate matter from uncertainty in the estimated half-life. Use the calculator at the ends of the range to see the spread.

    Is this a radiation safety or dose calculator?

    No. It calculates mathematical decay only. A remaining quantity is not a dose, an exposure or a safety assessment, and it ignores daughter products, detector efficiency and shielding. Radiation protection needs a qualified person and the applicable regulations.

    Where do the isotope half-lives in the preset menu come from, and are they exact?

    They are published values, listed with their sources and standard uncertainties in the Isotope half-lives section. They are not exact: evaluations differ in the last digits, and a preset fills only the half-life and its time unit. Use the value your own source or procedure requires, and remember that the calculator treats whatever half-life you enter as exact.

    Is anything sent to a server?

    No. The calculation runs in your browser, and the history list is stored only in your browser.
