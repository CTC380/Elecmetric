Worked example: transformer efficiency

ParameterResult
Input power100 kW
Output power96 kW
Power loss4 kW
Efficiency96%
Loss as a percentage of the input4%

The efficiency is the output divided by the input, 96%. The loss is the remaining 4 kW, which is 4% of the input. The two percentages add to 100% because they use the same reference.

Name the reference: one loss, three percentages

The same 4 kW loss, expressed againstReferenceResult
the input power100 kW4%
the output power96 kW≈ 4.166667%
a rated power120 kW≈ 3.333333%

A loss of 4 kW is 4% of the input, about 4.17% of the output and about 3.33% of a 120 kW rating. The calculation is the same each time. The reference is what changes, so the page asks you to name it.

Percentage or percentage points?

A power factor that rises from 0.80 to 0.90 goes from 80% to 90%. That is 10 pp, the absolute change. As a relative change it is a 12.5% increase. The two answers are different and both are correct. Mode 03 calculates both; From 80% to 90% is an increase of 10 percentage points (a relative increase of 12.5%).

Questions

How do I calculate transformer efficiency?

Divide the output power by the input power and multiply by 100. For 96 kW out and 100 kW in, 96 ÷ 100 × 100 = 96%. The loss is the difference, 4 kW, or 4% of the input.

What is the reference for each calculation?

The definitions table above names it for all eleven. Efficiency uses the input power, loading uses the rated capacity, voltage drop uses the source voltage, voltage deviation uses the nominal voltage, load factor uses the peak load, demand factor uses the connected load, derating uses the rated capacity, energy saving uses the previous consumption, and state of charge uses the usable capacity.

Why is the diversity factor not a percentage?

It is the sum of the individual maximum demands divided by the maximum demand of the group, a ratio of 1 or more, so the page shows it without a percent sign. It also shows the coincidence factor, the reciprocal, as a percentage.

What is the difference between voltage drop and voltage deviation?

The voltage drop is the loss along a circuit, as a share of the source voltage, and is never negative. The voltage deviation compares the actual voltage with the nominal voltage and keeps its sign: negative when the actual voltage is below nominal.

Can a result be above 100%?

Some can, such as a loading above 100% (an overload) or a derating that is an increase. The page accepts them and says what they mean. For an efficiency above 100% it adds a note, because a passive device cannot do that.

What is a negative energy saving?

The new consumption is higher than the previous one. The page shows the negative percentage, labels it an increase, and says it is not a saving.

Why are negative inputs refused, and what if the reference is 0?

Every quantity here is a size: a power, a voltage, a load, a capacity. A negative value is almost always a typing mistake. The page refuses it rather than calculate a meaningless percentage. What if the reference is 0? The page refuses it and names the quantity, because the percentage would divide by 0.

Do I enter units?

No. Enter plain numbers in one consistent unit for the two values, for example kW and kW, or A and A. The result is a percentage, so the unit cancels.

How large can the numbers be?

Each number can have up to 400 digits. The arithmetic uses whole numbers of unlimited length, not floating point, so the answer is exact at any size.

Is anything sent to a server?

Everything runs in your browser. The history of your last ten calculations is kept only in this browser and can be cleared with one button. This page also loads Google Tag Manager; data collected by tags depends on its configuration.
