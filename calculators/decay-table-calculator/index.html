Enter a half-life, and an initial quantity if you have one. The calculator gives a table of the percentage remaining at every half-life or every time step, an interactive decay curve on a linear or logarithmic scale, and a side-by-side comparison of up to three half-lives, with the working written out in your own numbers.     The formulas

    One decay law, Nₜ = N₀ × 2^(−t / T½), evaluated at evenly spaced times:

    The percentage remaining depends only on how many half-lives have passed. That is why every curve with the same half-life has the same shape, and why the initial quantity is optional.

    How to read the graph

    • The vertical axis is the percentage remaining; the horizontal axis is time in the unit shown. Dots mark the table rows of scenario A (up to 60 rows).
    • The curve falls steeply at first and flattens: the same fraction is lost in every half-life, so the amount lost shrinks.
    • On the logarithmic scale each halving is the same vertical step, so the decay is a straight line. A shorter half-life is a steeper line.
    • Curves with different half-lives cross only at time zero (both 100%).

    Rules and limits

    • The half-life must be greater than zero; the step must be greater than zero; the number of steps is a whole number from 1 to 200. The table always starts at time zero.
    • The initial quantity is optional; when entered it must be greater than zero. The quantity unit is a label only, and the percentages do not depend on it.
    • Each half-life has its own time unit, from nanoseconds to billions of years. A year is a Julian year of 365.25 days.
    • Results are double-precision. A remaining fraction below about 5 × 10⁻³²⁴ shows as 0% with a note that it is not exactly zero.
    • Mathematical decay only: not a radiation-safety, dosimetry or exposure assessment.

    Questions

    What does the decay table show?

    For each row it gives the time, the number of half-lives, the percentage remaining, the percentage decayed and, if you entered an initial quantity, the quantity left. With T½ = 5,730 yr the rows at 0, 1, 2 and 3 half-lives show 100%, 50%, 25% and 12.5%.

    Should I space the rows by half-lives or by time, and can I use a step smaller than one half-life?

    Half-lives give the familiar 100, 50, 25, 12.5 ladder, and whole steps are exact. Time gives rows at, for example, every year or every hour, which is what you need when you want the value at a particular date. Can I use a step smaller than one half-life? Yes. A step of 0.25 half-lives gives four rows per halving. After four quarter-steps the fraction is exactly 50%: 50% in the example for T½ 10 yr.

    How do I compare two or three half-lives, and can the scenarios use different units?

    Enter half-life B and, if you like, C. All scenarios are read at the same times, so each row shows every scenario side by side and the graph draws one curve each (solid, dashed and dotted, so the difference does not depend on colour). In the example, after 20 yr a 5-year half-life leaves 6.25% and a 10-year half-life leaves 25%. Can the scenarios use different units? Yes. Each half-life has its own unit and the time of every row is converted to it by exact unit ratios (a year is 365.25 days). After 30 days, a half-life of 8.02 days leaves 7.48088% and a half-life of 12 hours leaves 8.67362 × 10⁻¹⁷%.

    When should I use the logarithmic scale?

    When the quantity falls over many halvings. On a linear axis the curve is flat against the floor after about six half-lives; on a log axis each halving is the same vertical step, so an exponential decay is a straight line and you can read 40 half-lives (9.09495 × 10⁻¹¹%) on one chart.

    How does the interactive readout work?

    Move the slider, or hover or touch the graph. The dashed marker follows the curve and the line under the graph gives the half-lives, the percentage left, the percentage decayed and the quantity at that time for every scenario. The values are calculated from the model at that exact time, not read off the table.

    What can I download?

    The table as CSV (full-precision numbers), TXT or JSON, and the graph as SVG (sharp at any size) or PNG. The saved graph has a white background and fixed colours so it can go into a report or slide.

    Why do some rows show about 0%?

    A fraction below about 5 × 10⁻³²⁴ cannot be held in a double-precision number. It is shown as 0% with a note that it is not exactly zero, and the logarithmic graph is limited to 10⁻³⁰⁰ at its lower edge.

    Why is the number of steps limited to 200?

    A table of more than 200 rows is hard to read and the graph already shows the whole curve. For one value at one time use the Decay Percentage Calculator (Mode 03).

    Is this a radiation safety or dose calculator?

    No. It evaluates one exponential law. It does not give a dose, an exposure or a safety assessment, and it ignores any growth from a parent nuclide.

    Is anything sent to a server?

    No. The calculation and the graph are made in your browser, and the history list is stored only in your browser.
