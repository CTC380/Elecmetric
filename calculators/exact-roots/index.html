When a quadratic factors

Every quadratic ax² + bx + c with a ≠ 0 can be written as a(x − r₁)(x − r₂), where r₁ and r₂ are its roots. What changes is which numbers the factors need. If the discriminant Δ = b² − 4ac is the square of a rational number, the roots are rational and the quadratic factors with whole-number coefficients, such as (2x + 5)(x − 1) for 2x² + 3x − 5 = 0. If Δ is positive but not such a square, the factors need square roots, and the quadratic does not factor over the rationals. If Δ is negative, the factors contain i, and over the real numbers the quadratic stays in one piece.

A repeated root (Δ = 0) gives a perfect square, a(x − r)². The page also takes out any common factor first, so 3/4 x² + 1/2 x − 2 is factored as (1/4)(x + 2)(3x − 4).

The rational root theorem

Write the equation with whole-number coefficients. A rational root p/q, in lowest terms, must have p dividing the constant and q dividing the leading coefficient. For 2x² + 3x − 5 = 0 the constant is −5 and the leading coefficient is 2, so p is ±1 or ±5 and q is 1 or 2. That gives 8 candidates: ±1, ±5, ±1/2 and ±5/2. The page tests each one exactly, and x = 1 and x = −5/2 are the two that work. The theorem lists candidates; it does not say whether any works. The discriminant does: the roots are rational exactly when Δ is a perfect square, and the page checks that both routes agree.

Vertex form and completing the square

Completing the square rewrites ax² + bx + c as a(x − h)² + k, where h = −b/(2a) and k = c − b²/(4a). The point (h, k) is the vertex of the parabola. Setting the vertex form to zero gives (x − h)² = −k/a, and taking square roots gives x = h ± √(−k/a), which is the quadratic formula in another dress. Building an equation from its roots or its vertex

With the roots and A, the equation is A(x − r₁)(x − r₂) multiplied out: roots 1/3 and −5/2 with A = 1 give x² + (13/6)x − (5/6) = 0, which is 6x² + 13x − 5 = 0 in whole numbers. With a vertex (h, k) and A, the equation is A(x − h)² + k multiplied out: A = −2, vertex (3, 5) gives −2x² + 12x − 13 = 0. Either way the page then shows every form and checks that the equation solves back to what you entered. A complex pair of roots cannot be typed in; use the vertex form for those.

Exact, not rounded

Every coefficient is held as an exact fraction of whole numbers, so the factors are decided exactly. 0.1 is read as 1/10, not as a binary approximation, so 0.1x² + 0.2x + 0.1 is exactly (1/10)(x + 1)². Each form is expanded back to A, B and C and compared exactly, and all three forms are compared at nine values of x.

Questions

What is the difference between the factored form and the vertex form?

The factored form a(x − r₁)(x − r₂) shows where the curve crosses the x-axis. The vertex form a(x − h)² + k shows the turning point. Both describe the same parabola.

What if the quadratic does not factor over the integers?

Then its roots are irrational or complex. The page gives the factors with exact radicals, such as (x − 1 − √2)(x − 1 + √2), and says that they do not exist over the rationals.

Why is there a common factor outside the brackets?

Whole-number factors have no common factor, so any common factor of A, B and C, including a fraction, is taken out first. 6x² + 4x − 2 is 2(3x − 1)(x + 1).

What happens when A is zero?

The equation is linear, so there is nothing to factor. The page says so and gives the one root.

How do I use complex factors in a circuit calculation?

Mathematics writes the imaginary unit as i and electrical engineering writes it as j. Complex conjugate factors of a second-order circuit equation correspond to an oscillating, under-damped response.

Is anything sent to a server?

No. The calculation runs in your browser. The page’s security policy also blocks it from sending data to other sites, so nothing you enter leaves your device.
