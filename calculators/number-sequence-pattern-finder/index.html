How the pattern finder decides

Every number you enter is read exactly (a decimal such as 0.1 is the fraction 1/10, never a rounded binary number), so the tests below are exact equalities, not tolerances.

  • Finite differences. If the k-th differences are all equal, the sequence is a polynomial of degree k: constant (k = 0), arithmetic (1), quadratic (2), cubic (3) and so on. The formula comes from the Newton forward-difference form, then is expanded.
  • Ratios. Equal ratios of consecutive terms give a geometric sequence. A ratio of 0 and a ratio of 1 are handled separately, so there is no 0 ÷ 0 and no division by the sum formula’s zero denominator.
  • Exponential plus constant. If the first differences are themselves geometric, the rule is A·ρⁿ + C (for example 2ⁿ + 1).
  • Recurrences and named sequences. Second-order recurrences aₙ = p·aₙ₋₁ + q·aₙ₋₂ are solved from the terms; Fibonacci, Lucas, prime, factorial, Catalan, square, cube, triangular and similar numbers are matched anywhere in their run.
  • Other shapes. Alternating signs on a simple pattern, repeating cycles and two interleaved sequences.

A rule is only reported when it is supported by more terms than it needs to define itself, or when it exactly matches a named sequence. The page shows how many terms checked the rule.

One list, several rules

A finite list never fixes its own continuation. The list 1, 2, 4 is geometric (next term 8) and also fits the quadratic (n² − n + 2)/2 (next term 7). Both reproduce the three numbers, so the calculator lists both, ranks the better supported first and marks a rule that no term has independently checked as not validated.

The formulas

  • Differences: Δaₙ = aₙ₊₁ − aₙ; Δ²aₙ = Δaₙ₊₁ − Δaₙ; Δᵏ is the same step repeated. For a polynomial of degree k, Δᵏ is constant.
  • Newton form with the first term at index n₀: aₙ = Σ C(n − n₀, j)·Δʲan₀, summed from j = 0 to k.
  • Arithmetic: aₙ = an₀ + (n − n₀)d; sum of N terms Sₙ = (N/2)[2an₀ + (N − 1)d].
  • Geometric: aₙ = an₀·rⁿ⁻ⁿ⁰; Sₙ = a(1 − rᴺ)/(1 − r) for r ≠ 1, Sₙ = Na for r = 1; infinite sum a/(1 − r) when |r| < 1.
  • Recurrence: aₙ = p·aₙ₋₁ + q·aₙ₋₂ from two starting values.

Rules and limits

  • Indexing. The index of your first term is shown and can be set to 0, 1 or any whole number from −1000 to 1000. The formula and the nth term follow that choice.
  • Sequence versus series. The result rows keep them apart: the terms are the sequence; the “series sum” row adds the terms from the first index up to n.
  • Exact arithmetic, no tolerance. Integers, decimals and fractions are held as exact fractions. Because nothing is rounded, no tolerance is needed. The consequence: measured data that has been rounded (0.333, 0.667, 1.0) is not an exact pattern and will not be recognised; use a fitting tool for measurements.
  • Limits. 2 to 60 terms. Polynomial, arithmetic and geometric terms are computed directly for n up to 1,000,000,000 (very large geometric terms are shown as an approximation, not computed in full). Recurrence and named sequences are computed exactly up to about 5,000 steps. Series sums for other rules are exact up to 20,000 terms.
  • Prediction, not forecasting. This finds the exact rule behind a list of numbers. It is not statistical time-series forecasting and says nothing about noisy data.
  • Privacy. The calculation runs in your browser; nothing you type is sent anywhere.

Questions

How do I find the next number in a sequence?

Enter the numbers. The calculator tests for a constant difference, a constant ratio, constant higher differences, a recurrence and named sequences, and gives the next terms of the best supported rule. For 2, 6, 12, 20, 30 the second differences are all 2, so the rule is n² + n and the next term is 42.

How do I find the nth term formula?

For an arithmetic sequence use an₀ + (n − n₀)d. For a polynomial sequence use the Newton difference form and expand it. The calculator does both and shows the working under the result.

Why does it show more than one pattern?

Because more than one rule can reproduce the same finite list. The ranking prefers the rule that more terms agree with, and the alternatives are shown so you can check which one you mean.

What counts as a validated pattern?

A rule with p parameters needs p terms to be defined. Terms beyond those p are independent checks. A degree-k polynomial fitted to exactly k + 1 terms always fits and so proves nothing; it is shown as an exact fit and marked not validated.

What is the difference between a sequence and a series?

A sequence is the list of terms; a series is their sum. For 2, 5, 8, 11, 14 the 5th term is 14 and the series sum of the first five terms is 40.

Can I use fractions and negative numbers?

Yes. Enter 1/2, 1, 3/2, 2 or 10, 7, 4, 1 or 2, -4, 8, -16. Fractions stay exact in the result.
