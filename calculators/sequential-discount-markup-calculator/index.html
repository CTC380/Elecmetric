Why +10% and then −10% does not bring you back

StepWorking
Step 1Turn each percentage into a factor: divide it by 100 and add it to 1. +10% becomes 1.1; −10% becomes 0.9.
Step 2Change 1 (+10%): 100 × 1.1 = 110.
Step 3Change 2 (−10%): 110 × 0.9 = 99.
Step 4Multiply the factors to get the overall factor: 1.1 × 0.9 = 0.99. The overall change is (0.99 − 1) × 100 = −1%.
Step 5Check: 100 × 0.99 = 99, the same as the last change.

Each change is taken of a different value: 10% of 100 is 10, but 10% of 110 is 11. The overall change is −1%, not 0%, and adding the percentages (0%) is the mistake the page marks in every chain.

The original price after two discounts

StepWorking
Step 1Turn each discount into a factor: divide it by 100 and take it from 1. 20% off becomes 0.8; 10% off becomes 0.9.
Step 2Multiply the factors to get the overall factor: 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72. The total discount is (1 − 0.72) × 100 = 28%.
Step 3Divide the sale price by the overall factor: 72 ÷ 0.72 = 100.
Step 4Check: 100 → 80 → 72, which ends at the sale price you entered.

A product costs 72 after a 20% discount and then a further 10% off. The original price is 100, and the total discount is 28%, not the 30% you get by adding the two. Adding 30% to 72 gives 93.6, which is wrong: the discounts were taken off the original price, so they must be divided out of the sale price.

Markup and margin are not the same

StepWorking
Step 1Markup is a percentage of the cost. The profit is cost × markup: 80 × 0.25 = 20.
Step 2The selling price is cost plus profit: 80 + 20 = 100, the same as 80 × (1 + 0.25) = 80 × 1.25.
Step 3Margin is a percentage of the selling price: 20 ÷ 100 × 100 = 20%.
Step 4Check: the profit as a percentage of the cost is 25%, the markup you entered.

A cost of 80 with a 25% markup sells at 100, and the profit of 20 is 20% of that price. The two measure the same profit against different bases, so the margin is always the smaller number for a positive markup. To earn a 20% margin on a cost of 80 you charge 100, which is a 25% markup. Adding 20% to the cost gives 96, which earns less than 20%.

Questions

How do I apply two percentage changes in a row, and how do two successive discounts combine?

Turn each into a factor by dividing it by 100 and adding it to 1, then multiply the value by every factor. 100 up 10% and then down 10% is 100 × 1.1 × 0.9 = 99. How do two successive discounts combine? Multiply the factors. 20% off and then 10% off is 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72, so the total discount is 28%, not 30%.

Why is the overall change not the sum of the percentages?

Each percentage is taken of the value just before it, which is different each time. +10% then −10% adds up to 0%, but the real overall change is −1%. The page shows the sum beside the true change so the difference is visible.

Does the order of the changes matter?

Not for the final value: multiplication gives the same product in any order, so a 20% discount then 10% gives the same price as 10% then 20%. It does change the intermediate values, which the page lists.

How do I find the original price after a discount?

Divide the sale price by 1 minus the discount as a decimal. 920 after 20% off is 920 ÷ 0.8 = 1150. With several discounts, divide by the product of the factors: 72 after 20% and 10% off is 72 ÷ 0.72 = 100.

What is the difference between markup and margin?

Markup is profit as a percentage of the cost. Margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price. A 25% markup on a cost of 80 sells at 100 and is a 20% margin.

How do I turn a margin into a markup?

Markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin). A 20% margin is 0.2 ÷ 0.8 = a 25% markup. The reverse is margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup).

Can I use a negative percentage?

Yes. In a chain a minus sign is a fall and a plus sign a rise. In the discount questions a negative discount is a price rise, and the calculator tells you, in case it was a typing slip.

Why is a 100% margin refused?

A 100% margin would mean the profit is the whole selling price, so the cost would have to be 0. No selling price gives that margin on a cost above 0.

How long can a chain be?

Up to 30 percentages, and each number can have up to 400 digits. The arithmetic uses whole numbers of unlimited length, not floating point, so the answer is exact.
